# How I Think About Transparency
### Why clarity matters more than access

Transparency, to me, isn’t complicated or abstract. It’s face value. It’s about letting people see how things actually work so they’re not left confused, guessing, or filling in gaps on their own.

When people don’t understand what’s happening behind the scenes, confusion takes over — and confusion almost always turns into frustration or mistrust. That’s one of the main reasons I started sharing information publicly through a website, a Facebook page, and longer written explanations. Not to persuade or spin, but to help people understand processes that are often invisible or poorly explained.

In my view, transparency is about clarity of process and clarity of intent. When people can see how decisions are made, even if they disagree with the outcome, they’re less likely to assume something improper happened behind closed doors. Transparency doesn’t require agreement — it requires understanding.

I also recognize why transparency is such a sensitive topic when it comes to government. There have been real instances of bad intent in the past. That history matters. It shapes public perception and creates skepticism that doesn’t disappear just because current actors may be acting in good faith. Trust isn’t evaluated in isolation — it’s evaluated against memory.

At the same time, transparency also breaks down because of misunderstanding. From an early age, most people are taught a very generalized version of how government works. Those simplified models tend to stick, even though real-world governance is far more complex. Local, county, state, and federal systems are organized differently, overlap in unexpected ways, and operate with layers that aren’t obvious from the outside.

When people encounter real decisions or processes that don’t match the simplified version they were taught, things start to feel tangled and opaque. Even when information is technically available, without context it can feel convoluted rather than clarifying. In an environment where trust has already been damaged, complexity doesn’t just confuse — it reinforces suspicion.

That’s why I see transparency as more than releasing information. It’s about translation. It’s about explaining how things actually work in a way people can understand, so complexity doesn’t become a barrier to trust.

Personally, I feel responsible for contributing to transparency in the ways I already control. That means sharing information clearly when I can, explaining processes honestly, and helping people understand what’s happening rather than assuming they already know. I try to approach this gently — education works better than overload, and clarity works better than volume.

I don’t believe transparency should have an arbitrary stopping point. Openness should be the default. I’m skeptical of the idea that sharing more information creates harm or confusion on its own. In my experience, confusion usually comes from too little context, not too much information.

That said, I also recognize there are legally required exceptions. Certain matters — particularly those involving employees — must be handled in closed session. I don’t see that as a rejection of transparency, but as a procedural necessity. Even then, I believe transparency should resume as soon as it legally can, including the eventual release of closed-session minutes when appropriate.

Outside of those narrow requirements, I don’t believe transparency should be selectively withheld. To me, transparency isn’t a performance or a slogan. It’s a commitment to clarity, context, and good-faith communication — especially in systems that are already complex and historically distrusted.

If trust is going to be rebuilt, it won’t happen through secrecy or assumption. It starts when people can see the process, understand the intent, and feel confident that they aren’t being left in the dark.
