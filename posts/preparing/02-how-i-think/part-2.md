# How I Think About Disagreement
### Why slowing down matters more than winning

<img src="/assets/images/series-2/part-2.webp" alt="How I Think About Disagreement" class="centered-photo" />

Disagreement doesn’t start with positions for me — it starts with people.

When I realize I disagree with someone, my first instinct isn’t to prepare a rebuttal. Instead, I notice how invested they are. Are they confident? Emotionally attached? Speaking from experience? Or simply assertive by nature? That initial observation matters, because confidence can come from many places, and not all of them are about being right or wrong.

Because of that, my first internal move is to avoid getting defensive. Defensiveness shuts down understanding before it ever has a chance to form. Rather than reacting, I try to quickly internalize why this person might feel as strongly as they do. I mentally run through scenarios — what experiences, information, or values could be driving their confidence — and I look for a way to meet them where they are.

If I still believe they may be incorrect, I think carefully about how to present my perspective in a way that’s respectful and more likely to be heard. Disagreement, to me, is less about asserting dominance and more about understanding context.

When a disagreement doesn’t resolve quickly, I become even more cautious about continuing it in real time. Once both sides are clearly invested, pushing harder rarely leads anywhere productive. I’ve learned that standing face-to-face and trying to “win” a conversation usually just entrenches positions.

At that point, what keeps a conversation worth engaging is the ability to slow it down. I’m comfortable respectfully tabling a discussion so I can step back and do more research. If it feels appropriate, I may ask the other person to share sources or materials that inform their view — not as a challenge, but as a genuine attempt to understand how they arrived where they did.

Pausing a disagreement isn’t avoidance for me. It’s a way to protect the quality of the conversation and give it a better chance of being productive later.

Being misunderstood doesn’t change that approach. When I realize someone is hearing something different than what I intended, my instinct still isn’t to argue for argument’s sake. I try to understand why we’re missing each other. If the conversation remains calm, I’m willing to restate or reframe my point in a different way, searching for clarity rather than victory.

If that doesn’t seem to help, I’m comfortable pausing again. I may offer to share materials that shaped my thinking so the other person can see my perspective in its full context. Misunderstanding, to me, isn’t a failure — it’s a signal to slow down or step back, not escalate.

Once a disagreement ends, I don’t carry it forward. My military experience taught me how to mentally disengage when a situation is over — almost like flipping a light switch. If the agreement was to revisit the topic later, I honor that without pushing. Pressing an unresolved issue usually makes things worse, not better.

I’m open to re-engaging if the other person wants to return to the discussion. But I’m also clear about boundaries. If a conversation shifts from a healthy debate into emotional conflict, I’m willing to pause again and suggest coming back to it later, when emotions aren’t driving the exchange.

For me, disagreement doesn’t need to end in agreement to be handled well. What matters is respect, clarity, and the discipline to know when to engage, when to pause, and when to move on.
