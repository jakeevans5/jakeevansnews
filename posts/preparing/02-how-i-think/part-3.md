# How I Think About Listening to the Public
### Why understanding comes before responding

Listening, to me, isn’t about waiting for my turn to speak. It’s about making sure I actually understand what someone is trying to tell me — without misinterpretation, assumption, or unnecessary filtering.

When someone from the public brings a concern or opinion to me, the first thing I’m focused on is clarity. Before facts, before solutions, before responses, I want to be confident that I understand what they mean — not just the words they’re using, but the concern underneath them. A lot of misunderstanding happens not because people disagree, but because they’re talking past each other without realizing it.

To avoid that, I keep things natural. I don’t rely on formal techniques or scripted responses. If something isn’t clear, I’ll restate what I heard in my own words or ask follow-up questions. Most of the time, understanding comes simply from continuing the conversation — talking through the issue until we get to the root of it. Listening, for me, is active and conversational, not procedural.

Over time, I’ve also learned something important: when people bring concerns or opinions forward, they usually do so because they believe they’re talking to someone who will listen. If I immediately jump into arguing — even when I disagree — that trust can disappear quickly. Earlier in my life, I was more inclined to debate right away. Experience has taught me that most people feel they’re right for a reason, and that reason deserves to be understood before it’s challenged.

Because of that, I pay close attention to why someone is talking to me. If they’re looking for feedback and want my perspective, I’m willing to offer it honestly — whether I agree or disagree. But if they’re simply needing to be heard, I don’t force disagreement into the conversation. In my experience, most people aren’t asking to be argued with. They’re asking to know that someone is actually listening.

Listening doesn’t stop when the conversation ends. Afterward, what matters to me is that the person walks away knowing their concern wasn’t just acknowledged, but respected. Even if no immediate solution exists, I want them to know I understood them and took them seriously.

Internally, I continue working to understand where they’re coming from. I don’t dismiss concerns just because I disagree with them. If there’s legitimacy or justification in what someone raises — whether I personally agree with it or not — I’m willing to explore it further. Representation, to me, isn’t conditional on alignment. If I represent someone in any way, I believe I owe them honest consideration and effort.

That mindset isn’t new for me. Whether I was serving as a supervisor, a sergeant in the military, or in other leadership roles, I’ve always believed that when someone brings something to your attention, you have a responsibility to understand it fully. Agreement isn’t required for respect. What matters is acting in good faith and working on their behalf when responsibility exists.

Listening, at its core, is about trust. It’s about creating space where people feel safe enough to speak honestly — and knowing that being heard is often the first step toward anything else that follows.
