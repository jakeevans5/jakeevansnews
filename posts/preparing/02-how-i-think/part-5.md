# How I Think About Being Wrong and Course-Correcting
### Why learning from mistakes matters more than avoiding them

<img src="/assets/images/series-2/part-5.jpg" alt="How I Think About Being Wrong and Course-Correcting" class="centered-photo" />

Being wrong doesn’t bother me the way it seems to bother some people. I don’t attach embarrassment, guilt, or defensiveness to it. I see being wrong — and correcting myself — as part of being human. History is full of genuinely great people who were wrong about things they believed were right. I don’t see myself as above that.

What matters to me isn’t avoiding being wrong. It’s what happens when I realize I might be.

My first instinct is to get to the truth as quickly as possible. If I become aware that my understanding is incomplete or incorrect, I don’t sit on that information. Especially when someone else is relying on what I’ve said, I feel a responsibility to surface doubt immediately. In work settings, for example, if I’ve given information to a supervisor and later realize it may not be right, I’ll say so directly. I’ll explain what triggered the doubt — new information, conflicting details, or something that doesn’t add up — and I’ll be clear that I’m researching to confirm the correct answer. Then I follow through and close the loop.

That approach isn’t about image or self-protection. It’s about accuracy and responsibility. Decisions get made based on the information people have. Letting uncertainty linger quietly is far more damaging than acknowledging it openly.

Admitting I was wrong doesn’t feel difficult to me. I don’t experience it as a loss or a setback. I’m comfortable saying, “I got this wrong,” because the goal isn’t to be right — it’s to get it right. Clarifying and correcting feels like progress, not failure.

That doesn’t mean I treat mistakes lightly. When I realize I was wrong, I care deeply about understanding how I got there. I want to know what assumptions I made, what information I relied on, and where the reasoning broke down. That reflection matters to me because it reduces the chance of repeating the same mistake in the future, when possible. Learning the path matters as much as correcting the destination.

When it comes to correcting course, I don’t believe in one-size-fits-all rules. Context matters. If a situation is serious or time-sensitive, getting ahead of it quickly is critical. Delay can compound harm or create new problems. In those moments, speed matters.

At the same time, I’m careful not to rush a correction just for the sake of speed. If I’ve already shared incorrect information once, the worst outcome would be introducing more confusion while trying to fix it. I want corrections to be clear, accurate, and complete — not half-answers that need further undoing. Accuracy and clarity matter just as much as timeliness.

Where I do carry weight isn’t around personal discomfort — it’s around impact. If a mistake or decision I made affected other people, I feel a responsibility to try to address that impact. If there’s a way to fix or mitigate harm, I believe it’s important to pursue it. If there isn’t, I still feel obligated to be honest about what happened and why.

I don’t expect people to judge me by the absence of mistakes. What I hope they see instead is a consistent pattern: a willingness to acknowledge error, correct course, understand failures well enough not to repeat them, and act in good faith toward the people affected.

Being wrong isn’t something I carry emotionally. But learning from it — and taking responsibility for its consequences — is something I take seriously.
