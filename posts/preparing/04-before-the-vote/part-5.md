# What I Deliberately Leave Until the Meeting
### Letting understanding come before conclusions

<img src="/assets/images/series-4/part-5.jpg" alt="How I Think About Being Wrong and Course-Correcting" class="centered-photo" />

I don’t usually walk into meetings trying to hold conclusions back — because I don’t try to form them early in the first place.

If the agenda or packet clearly answers the basic questions — who, what, when, where, why, and how — then I can usually understand the issue before the meeting begins. In those cases, there’s often nothing left to resolve other than confirming that understanding during discussion.

If those answers aren’t clear from the packet, that’s when I form questions instead of conclusions. I use the meeting itself to listen for those answers during the presentation or discussion. Once I understand what’s being proposed, why it’s being brought forward, who it affects, and how it’s intended to work, that’s when I’m able to form an opinion.

That sequence matters to me. Questions come before conclusions, not the other way around.

Approaching meetings this way helps keep decisions grounded in shared information rather than assumptions. It ensures that understanding is built in the room, not ahead of it, and that conclusions are based on what’s actually discussed — not what was assumed beforehand.
