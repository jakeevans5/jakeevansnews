# How I Read Agendas and Packets
### Finding context before details

I always start with the agenda.

Before I open a packet, I want to understand what’s actually being brought forward and where each item sits in the process. My first pass through the agenda is about orientation, not judgment. I scan for anything that’s out of the ordinary — new items I haven’t seen before, emergency readings, town halls, or anything that breaks the normal rhythm of a meeting.

Once I’ve identified those items, I move into the packet to see what’s behind them. If something is labeled as an emergency, I want to understand what the emergency is and why it needs to be expedited. If it’s a new item, I’m looking to understand what’s being introduced and what prompted it. If it’s a town hall, I want to know the reason for it and what discussion it’s meant to support.

I make multiple passes, but not to catch mistakes. When I refer to first, second, or final readings, I’m paying attention to where an item sits in the formal process. A first reading tells me this is being introduced. A second or final reading tells me it’s been discussed before and that a decision point is approaching.

At that stage, my focus changes. I’m no longer learning something from scratch — I’m comparing. I’m looking at what has changed since the last meeting and, more importantly, why. If revisions were made, I want to understand what discussion led to those changes and whether the language in the packet accurately reflects what was discussed publicly.

I also pay close attention to financials, especially anything that appears to add to or adjust the budget. Even small additions matter if they create long-term obligations or future commitments.

This process helps me stay grounded. I’m not trying to jump ahead, read intent into outcomes, or assume authority I don’t have. I’m simply trying to understand what’s being asked, where it sits in the sequence, and whether the documentation matches the conversation that brought it forward.
