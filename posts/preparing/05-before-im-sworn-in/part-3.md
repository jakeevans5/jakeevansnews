# How I Think About Accountability
### Due Diligence Before Authority

Before I’m sworn in, accountability looks like due diligence—nothing more and nothing less.

It means learning an issue thoroughly, studying its background, and grounding my understanding in empirical evidence whenever possible. If a law, the charter, or existing code applies, accountability means following it. Not because it aligns with my personal views, but because it already exists.

I tend to think about accountability in very black-and-white terms. If it’s a rule, it’s a rule. Whether I agree with it or not doesn’t change that obligation. It doesn’t feel personal, and it doesn’t hang over me emotionally. It simply is.

Before I’m sworn in, that’s true in the same way it’s true for any citizen. You follow the rules. And if there’s something you disagree with, the appropriate response isn’t to bend them—it’s to engage through the proper channels. That might mean reaching out to a ward representative or an at-large council member to understand whether there’s a lawful path forward.

Even looking ahead, accountability doesn’t mean unilateral action. City Council doesn’t directly manage departments—that responsibility rests with the City Manager. Authority is structured, limited, and clearly defined. Understanding those boundaries is part of accountability itself.

So for me, accountability isn’t about pressure, optics, or emotion. It’s about respecting the framework that already exists, doing the work to understand it fully, and accepting that personal agreement is not the standard.

That mindset doesn’t begin at swearing-in. It begins before it.
