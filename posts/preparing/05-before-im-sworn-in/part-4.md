# How I Approach Uncertainty and Gray Areas
### Exhausting What’s Knowable Before Accepting What Isn’t

<img src="/assets/images/series-5/part-4.jpeg" alt="Empty Room" class="centered-photo" />

Before I’m sworn in, uncertainty doesn’t feel like something to resolve. It feels like something to understand.

When I come across something that isn’t clearly defined, my approach is pretty methodical. If it’s a question a city employee might reasonably know the answer to, I’ll ask them. And when I do, I’m often just as interested in how they know the answer as the answer itself, because understanding the source matters.

If that doesn’t provide clarity, I’ll research it on my own. Sometimes that means using AI tools. Other times it means looking at how other towns handle similar situations to see whether there’s a common approach or precedent that adds context.

If the answer still isn’t clear, I may ask a council member to see if they can help track it down or point me in the right direction. Not to shortcut the process, but to better understand where an answer should come from when it isn’t obvious.

And once I’ve exhausted the resources I know of, that’s where I stop.

At that point, I’m comfortable acknowledging that I’m dealing with a gray area. Not everything has a clean answer, and not every uncertainty needs to be forced into one—especially before any authority exists at all.

For me, recognizing the limits of what’s knowable is just as important as doing the work to understand what is.
