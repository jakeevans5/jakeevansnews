# Why Some Decisions Take Time
### How required steps shape the pace of decisions

<img src="/assets/images/series-3/part-4.webp" alt="Council Agenda" class="centered-photo" />

For a long time, when I saw something taking a while to move through city government, I assumed the simplest explanation.

I assumed city council was being lazy.
Or that they didn’t want to deal with the issue.
Or that it just wasn’t important enough to act on.

Looking back, that assumption says more about what I didn’t understand than about what was actually happening.

What I’ve learned is that delays in local government are very often the result of required process, not lack of effort.

There are steps—often multiple ones—that legally have to be followed before certain actions can be taken. Those steps aren’t optional, and they aren’t something council can just bypass because something feels urgent or obvious. In many cases, those requirements were set in motion years ago by elected officials who came before—or even directly by voters through ballot measures.

That means today’s council members are often operating inside a framework they didn’t create but are still obligated to follow.

Public notice requirements, readings of ordinances, waiting periods, legal reviews, budget cycles, and coordination with outside entities all add time. What can look like “nothing is happening” from the outside is often a sequence of steps quietly moving forward behind the scenes.

Understanding that has changed how I interpret delay.

It doesn’t mean every process is perfect. It doesn’t mean timelines can’t be improved. But it does mean that what looks like inaction is often compliance.

It’s also made me reflect on something else: why those processes exist in the first place.

Many of them were created with good intentions—transparency, accountability, public input, and legal protection. At the time they were adopted, they likely made sense for the tools, technology, and pace of life that existed then.

Now, with modern communication, faster information flow, and different public expectations, it’s fair to ask whether some of those processes still serve their original purpose as effectively as they once did.

That’s not a criticism. It’s a question.

Learning this has made me more patient with the process, but also more thoughtful about how systems evolve. It’s helped me separate frustration with timing from frustration with intent.

I’m still learning where patience is required and where reevaluation might be appropriate. But I no longer assume that slow movement means no one cares or no one is working.

Sometimes, it simply means the system is doing exactly what it was designed to do—whether or not it still fits the world we live in today.
