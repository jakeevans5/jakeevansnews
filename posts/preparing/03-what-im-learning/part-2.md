# Council vs. Staff Roles
### Why understanding who does what changes how people engage


Before I started paying close attention to local government, I assumed it worked like a smaller version of the federal system.

In my head, there was a legislative branch, an executive branch, and a judicial branch—just with different titles. I assumed the city council functioned like a local legislature, passing laws that were then sent to an executive branch, led by the mayor, to be signed and enforced. I also assumed the municipal court played a role similar to federal courts, just at the city level.

I knew that different cities used different titles—some had council members, others had aldermen—but I assumed that was mostly cultural. Different names, same structure.

What I’ve learned since is that this mental model doesn’t really hold up, at least not in our city.

Neosho operates under a council–city manager form of government. That means the structure isn’t divided cleanly into legislative and executive branches the way I expected. The mayor isn’t elected citywide in the way a president or governor is. Instead, the mayor is selected from among the council members, and day-to-day operations are handled by professional staff.

That distinction alone changes a lot.

Council’s role is largely about setting policy, approving ordinances, and providing direction. Staff’s role is to operate the city, carry out those policies, and manage the daily work. Those roles are connected, but they’re not interchangeable—and confusing them creates real problems.

One of the biggest things this has changed for me is how I think about accountability.

A lot of frustration directed at “the city” comes from people going to the wrong place with a concern—not because they’re careless, but because they’re operating under a reasonable assumption about how government works. When an issue doesn’t get resolved, it can feel like no one is listening or no one cares, when in reality the concern may simply be landing in the wrong lane.

Over time, that disconnect muddies the water. People want to be involved. They want to raise concerns. But when the system feels confusing, the learning curve itself becomes a barrier. Engagement starts to feel more like irritation than participation.

Learning this has changed how I respond when someone brings an issue to me.

Because I know I’m still learning myself, I’m much more careful about answering too quickly. Before responding, I try to get as much clarity as I can. I’ll look through the city charter or code to see whether something is clearly addressed there. If it isn’t, I’ll ask questions or look for context before offering guidance.

A good example of this is street maintenance. There are several roads that run through Neosho that are actually owned and maintained by the state, not the city. It’s completely reasonable for someone to assume the city can fix a pothole on a road inside city limits—but legally, the city often can’t. Even if they want to, they’re not allowed to.

Without that context, the city ends up getting blamed for something that’s outside its authority. With that context, the frustration doesn’t disappear—but it becomes clearer where responsibility actually lies.

I’ve also seen how understanding roles can help people get better results. In one case involving a concern about an aggressive dog, I encouraged someone to bring the issue to city council. That led to follow-up they’d been waiting on and eventually resulted in an ordinance being clarified to better address their situation.

I don’t share that as a success story, but as an example of how clearer pathways make participation more effective.

Understanding council versus staff roles hasn’t made me less sympathetic to frustration—it’s made me more patient. It’s helped me listen differently, respond more carefully, and focus on helping people reach the right place rather than just validating how confusing the system can feel.

I’m still learning where those lines are and how they work in practice. But I’ve learned enough to know that a lot of civic frustration isn’t about apathy or bad intent—it’s about misunderstanding roles in a system that isn’t intuitive unless you take time to study it.

And that learning, for me, is still ongoing.
