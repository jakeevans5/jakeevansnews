# What I’m Learning, Part 3: Budgets, Constraints, and Tradeoffs
### Why funding decisions aren’t as flexible as they appear

![Series 3 – Part 3 header image](assets/images/series-3/part-3.jpeg)

Going into this, I felt like I already had a decent understanding of how city budgets worked.

At a high level, the process made sense to me. City council approves a budget, departments operate within it, and spending is guided by what’s been formally adopted. That part wasn’t new or surprising.

What I’m learning now isn’t that my understanding was wrong—it’s that it was incomplete.

The biggest shift for me hasn’t been about how budgets are approved. It’s been about understanding how money actually enters the system in the first place, and how many limits exist before any spending decisions are even on the table.

Like most people, I understood that cities collect revenue through taxes. What I didn’t fully appreciate was how constrained that revenue can be. City council can’t simply raise taxes on its own. Any increase has to go through a public vote. That alone puts a very real boundary around what’s possible, regardless of priorities or needs.

I’m also learning more about bonds—how they work, how they’re approved, and the obligations that come with them. Bonds can fund important projects, but they’re not free money. They’re tied to specific purposes, repayment schedules, and legal requirements that limit flexibility.

Even once money is collected, it often isn’t interchangeable.

Some revenue is legally earmarked for specific uses. A good example is utility-related revenue. Money collected through water rates, for instance, is typically restricted to operating, maintaining, or improving the water system itself. Even if there were excess revenue, it generally can’t just be redirected to unrelated needs like streets or parks.

Those restrictions exist for accountability reasons—but they also mean that “just move the money” is rarely as simple as it sounds.

Learning this has changed how I hear common suggestions like, “The city should just fund this,” or “Why don’t they take money from somewhere else?” Those ideas aren’t unreasonable—but they often assume a level of flexibility that doesn’t exist in practice.

Because of that, I’ve become much more cautious in how I frame questions and expectations around city spending. Before assuming something should be possible, I now find myself asking whether it’s even legally or structurally allowed. Are the funds restricted? Was the revenue voter-approved for a specific purpose? Are there long-term obligations tied to it?

That doesn’t mean questions shouldn’t be asked. It means they need to be the right questions.

I’m still learning the details, and I don’t pretend to have mastered all of it. But understanding these constraints has helped me better appreciate why tradeoffs exist—and why budgeting is often less about choosing between good and bad options and more about choosing between competing priorities inside a very narrow lane.

That awareness has made me slower to judge and more careful to listen.

And for me, that’s been one of the most important lessons so far.
